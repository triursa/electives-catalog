# Argument practice and writing guide

Write in your own document workspace. Use the PHIL 101 syllabus for the brief and resources, then keep a link or index to your work wherever you prefer.

## A repeatable reading method

1. Read the assigned passage once for its question and conclusion.
2. Read again for reasons. Number the premises and name any assumption the author leaves unstated.
3. Identify a passage or section in your edition. Distinguish a quotation from your paraphrase.
4. Formulate the strongest objection you can, then consider a charitable reply.
5. Close the source and reconstruct the argument from memory. Reopen it and correct your account.

Save notes in `01 Reading Notes`. Use one document per week or author, whichever is easier to return to. Reading estimates include close reading; the separate consolidation task is for revisiting difficulties and correcting notes.

## Early argument exercises

Attempt these in your notes before expanding the feedback. These are original practice examples, not quotations from the course authors. Use OpenStax Chapter 5 alongside them.

### A. Find the structure

Every claim supported only by an unreliable source needs further checking. This claim is supported only by an unreliable source. Therefore this claim needs further checking.

Number the premises and conclusion. Is the argument deductively valid? What else would you need to know before calling it sound?

<details><summary>Feedback for A</summary>

Premise 1 supplies the general conditional; premise 2 says the particular claim meets its condition. The conclusion follows by applying that conditional. The argument is valid. Soundness additionally requires true premises: evaluate both the proposed checking principle and whether this claim really has only that source. Validity alone does not establish either premise.

</details>

### B. Test the inference

If an argument is sound, it is valid. This argument is valid. Therefore it is sound.

Explain the mistake and give a counterexample using the same structure.

<details><summary>Feedback for B</summary>

This affirms the consequent. A counterexample with the same form is: if an animal is a robin, it is a bird; this sparrow is a bird; therefore it is a robin. The premises are true and the conclusion false. A valid argument can also have a false premise. For example: all birds are mammals; all robins are birds; therefore all robins are mammals. The structure is valid, but the first premise is false, so the argument is not sound. The original inference reverses a one-way implication.

</details>

### C. Compare types of support

The last ten buses on this route arrived on time, so tomorrow's bus will probably arrive on time. A bus is late and traffic is unusually heavy, so traffic may explain the delay.

Which inference predicts from observed cases? Which proposes an explanation? Identify one way each could fail.

<details><summary>Feedback for C</summary>

The first is an inductive projection. The sample may be unrepresentative, or conditions may change. The second is abductive: heavy traffic is a candidate explanation, but a mechanical problem might explain the same observation. Neither establishes its conclusion with deductive necessity. Strength depends on relevant evidence and alternatives.

</details>

### D. Expose a hidden premise

This tradition has existed for centuries; therefore we should keep following it.

Supply a bridging premise, then challenge it without assuming that old traditions are always bad.

<details><summary>Feedback for D</summary>

One possible bridge is that long survival is sufficient reason to continue a practice. That is contestable: survival may reflect coercion or circumstance rather than value. A better argument needs evidence about the practice's benefits and costs. Rejecting the inference does not prove the practice should end; that also requires reasons.

</details>

## Reading-response model excerpt

The following is a short illustration of argument, objection, and revision. It is not a response to submit or a single correct interpretation.

In Plato's *Euthyphro*, a proposed account connects piety with what all the gods love. Socrates asks whether piety is loved because it is pious, or is pious because it is loved. If the gods love pious actions because those actions are already pious, their love responds to a feature rather than explaining what that feature is. On that reading, universal divine approval may identify pious actions without defining piety itself.

An objection is that a reliable way to identify piety could be enough for practical decision-making. Someone deciding what to do may care more about recognizing an action than about its explanation. Socrates' criticism is therefore strongest if we accept that a definition should explain what makes a thing pious, not merely give a dependable sign of it.

I find that demand reasonable for the dialogue's inquiry, but it needs defending. A next question is whether any proposed definition must explain a property, or whether some useful definitions work by reliable identification alone.

**Why this works:** It identifies the relevant distinction, reconstructs a reason, raises an objection, and narrows its conclusion. In your own response, add the passage/section reference from your edition and verify the surrounding context. See [Plato's dialogue](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1642/pg1642-images.html).

## Essay and final-paper rubric

Use this rubric for formative self-review. A well-supported disagreement with the author can score as highly as a defense. An introductory paper does not need a historically novel thesis.

| Criterion | Strong | Developing | Revise |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thesis | Specific, arguable answer to a focused question | Position is present but broad | Topic summary replaces a position |
| Reconstruction | Premises and conclusion are accurate and charitable | Mostly accurate but key steps are unclear | Argument is distorted or only named |
| Evidence | Relevant passage references support the interpretation | References need explanation or precision | Assertions lack textual support |
| Objection and reply | Serious challenge is answered with reasons | Objection is present but response is weak | Objection is dismissed or absent |
| Clarity | Each paragraph advances the argument | Some repetition or gaps | Reader cannot follow the reasoning |

For each criterion, write one sentence explaining your judgment and one revision you can make. Revise before marking the work complete. Cite editions, translators/adapters, section numbers, and all outside help. Use assistance to clarify and test reasoning; write your own argument.

## Final-paper stages

Use the tasks in Weeks 9–15: proposal, sources, outline, draft, feedback, revision, and final polish. These stages total 20 planned hours. The complete paper is 1,500–2,000 words and engages at least two assigned philosophers. Save the evolving draft in `05 Final Paper`, then export a final snapshot if useful.

## Weekly review

Before reopening notes, reconstruct an argument from this week and one from an earlier week. Compare both with their sources. Record a correction, a change in your view, and a question for a later review in `06 Reflection and Progress`.

The purpose is to make understanding visible. A checked task or an hour total records activity; the arguments, corrections, and revisions in your documents show your learning.
