# PHIL 210: Albert Camus, Absurdism and Revolt

Version 1 · 6 weeks · 23.83–29.25 h of required work

A six-week mini course on the philosophy of Albert Camus, tracing the arc from absurdism in The Myth of Sisyphus through the literary expression of the absurd in The Stranger, to collective revolt and political ethics in The Plague and Neither Victims nor Executioners. Read Camus in his own words and engage with his central questions: Why should I not kill myself? Why should I not kill others? What does it mean to live without appeal?

**All required materials are freely available online.** No purchase or paid subscription is needed.

This is an independent study plan. It does not award academic credit or a credential. The workload averages 4.4 hours per week; use the weekly ranges to plan your time. Stretch a week when a text needs more attention. Optional work is excluded from those totals.

## What you will learn

- Explain what Camus means by 'the absurd' and how it differs from nihilism and existentialism.
- Reconstruct Camus's argument against suicide in The Myth of Sisyphus and evaluate its soundness.
- Interpret The Stranger as a literary enactment of absurd consciousness, identifying key philosophical moments.
- Trace the development from individual absurd revolt to collective political revolt in The Plague.
- Articulate Camus's ethic of 'neither victims nor executioners' and assess its relevance to contemporary political violence.
- Compare Camus's rejection of systematic philosophy with Sartrean existentialism and assess the Camus-Sartre split.
- Defend your own position on whether Camus's response to the absurd is livable, with textual evidence.
