# ECON 260: Behavioral Economics

Version 1 · 15 weeks · 116.25–147 h of required work

An intermediate survey of behavioral economics, tracing the field from Herbert Simon's bounded rationality through Kahneman and Tversky's heuristics-and-biases program, prospect theory, intertemporal choice, social preferences, and Thaler and Sunstein's nudge theory. Students engage with primary sources, classic experiments, and real-world applications in finance, public policy, and everyday decision-making.

**Some required materials require purchase or library access.** This course assigns books that are not freely available online. Check the task resource list for each week before starting.

This is an independent study plan. It does not award academic credit or a credential. The workload averages 8.8 hours per week; use the weekly ranges to plan your time. Stretch a week when a text needs more attention. Optional work is excluded from those totals.

## What you will learn

- Explain the historical emergence of behavioral economics and its challenge to the rational-actor model.
- Identify and apply key heuristics (availability, representativeness, anchoring) and their associated biases.
- Compare prospect theory to expected utility theory, explaining loss aversion, reference dependence, and probability weighting.
- Analyze intertemporal choice problems including present bias, hyperbolic discounting, and self-control strategies.
- Evaluate the role of social preferences (fairness, reciprocity, conformity) in economic decision-making.
- Apply nudge theory and choice architecture principles to real-world policy and business problems.
- Critically assess the ethics of libertarian paternalism and common critiques of behavioral economics.
- Design a behavioral intervention for a self-selected problem and evaluate its expected effectiveness.
