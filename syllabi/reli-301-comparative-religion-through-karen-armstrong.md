## Compact syllabus: outcomes and evidence

1. Trace Karen Armstrong's biographical journey and explain how her personal experience shapes her comparative method.
2. Define and apply the distinction between mythos and logos as Armstrong frames it.
3. Compare the development of monotheism across Judaism, Christianity, and Islam using Armstrong's historical framework.
4. Explain the Axial Age concept and evaluate its usefulness as a comparative tool across Chinese, Indian, Greek, and Israelite traditions.
5. Analyze Armstrong's argument that fundamentalism is a modern phenomenon rather than a return to premodern religion.
6. Evaluate Armstrong's claim that religion is not the primary cause of violence and that the secular state has been more destructive.
7. Distinguish premodern allegorical scriptural reading from modern literalism and explain why Armstrong considers literalism a distortion.
8. Articulate Armstrong's vision of compassion as the essence of religion and assess it critically.
9. Synthesize Armstrong's major themes into a coherent understanding of comparative religion as a discipline.

Outcome evidence: 1 is practiced in Week 1; 2 in Week 2; 3 in Weeks 3–6 and Essay 1; 4 in Weeks 7–9 and Essay 2; 5 in Week 10; 6 in Week 12; 7 in Week 13; 8 in Week 14; 9 in the final paper and Week 15 reflection. Weekly responses and source records supply evidence for these outcomes. Agreement with Armstrong is not a success criterion: accurate reconstruction and reasoned evaluation are.

## How to use a study week

Read the weekly question and activity list first. Split reading into 30–60-minute sessions. Start the next session by recalling yesterday’s central claim before reopening the text. Write notes while reading; draft responses only after collecting evidence. End the week with the closing check and record your next action.

Time estimates include reading, note-making, planning, writing, and the required self-checks. The first activity’s estimate also includes 20–40 minutes for weekly navigation, recall, and closing review. The longer books use bounded thematic selections, not complete-book assignments. A Short History of Myth and Twelve Steps assign the complete main text and have larger reading budgets. Optional AI conversations and additional reading are excluded; reserve another 15–30 minutes per AI review if you choose it. Stretch any week rather than rushing reading or revisions to meet an estimate. These are planning estimates, not measured completion times.

For a thematic selection, inspect the contents and index, select the stated number of pages across the named topics, and write down the headings and exact ranges before reading. Finish the final paragraph or section. If you cannot locate one theme, record that gap and adjust within the page budget; do not invent an example. Page counts guide workload across editions rather than specifying a universal edition. Audiobook users should map the same sections and cite chapter plus timestamp; total audiobook runtime is not the assigned workload.

No threshold is a pass, no percentage or letter grade should be calculated, and disagreement with Armstrong is not penalized when argued fairly.

Use “Not assessable” when the required draft, source material, or context is unavailable. Do not turn missing reviewer access into zero. Use zero only when the submitted work is assessable and lacks the criterion. Give an equal-weight total out of 12 only when all four criteria can be assessed; otherwise omit the total without rescaling. Source accuracy cannot be confirmed from a draft citation alone. Separate assessment of citation form from verification against source text.

### Rubric A: short analytical responses

1. **Position and task fit:** 0 = no answer to the question; 1 = implied or mostly summary; 2 = clear answer addressing the main request; 3 = precise, qualified answer addressing every required part.
2. **Comparison, distinction, or alternative:** 0 = required comparison or objection absent; 1 = mentioned without explanation; 2 = relevant distinction or alternative explained fairly; 3 = weighed through specific evidence with a clear implication. For Response 1 this means insight versus bias; for other responses use the distinction or objection in that activity’s brief.
3. **Evidence:** 0 = no relevant evidence in an otherwise assessable draft; 1 = vague example or incomplete locator; 2 = specific relevant passage with usable locator and explanation; 3 = contextualized, verified evidence with its limitations acknowledged. Mark source verification Not assessable when the source is unavailable.
4. **Reasoning and clarity:** 0 = assertion without reasoning; 1 = some reasoning with major gaps; 2 = coherent link between evidence and answer; 3 = clear, economical reasoning that handles a serious objection or uncertainty.

### Rubric B: comparative essays and the research paper

1. **Thesis and scope:** 0 = no controlling claim; 1 = broad topic or descriptive claim; 2 = arguable thesis within the brief’s scope; 3 = precise thesis sustained throughout the paper with appropriate qualifications.
2. **Comparison and critical engagement:** 0 = required cases absent; 1 = parallel summaries with little analysis; 2 = comparison on a shared dimension and a relevant objection; 3 = similarities and differences interpreted in context, with a substantive objection answered and limits stated.
3. **Source use and integrity:** 0 = required sources or evidence absent from an assessable paper; 1 = sources listed but weakly used or citations incomplete; 2 = required sources support specific claims with traceable citations; 3 = sources are accurately contextualized, quotations/paraphrases verified, and outside-source credibility considered. Verification is Not assessable without sufficient source material.
4. **Structure and revision:** 0 = no usable argument structure; 1 = organization obscures reasoning; 2 = logical sections, clear prose, and at least one documented revision; 3 = purposeful progression, well-integrated evidence and counterargument, and revisions that repair a substantive weakness.

### Rubric C: source notes and project preparation

For notes, use the “notes” interpretation; for the proposal/outline use “project” below.

1. **Required coverage:** 0 = required items absent; 1 = some required cases/items missing; 2 = all requested items present; 3 = all present with a clear connection to the activity question. Project: the proposal/outline includes the required question, sections, and source counts.
2. **Accuracy and traceability:** 0 = unsupported or untraceable content; 1 = locators or attribution incomplete; 2 = claims attributed with usable locators; 3 = evidence checked in context, with uncertainty labeled. Not assessable when checking requires unavailable source text. Project: sources are obtainable and matched to claims; a future evidence gap is explicitly labeled.
3. **Interpretation or feasibility:** 0 = no explanation or feasible plan; 1 = generic interpretation/plan; 2 = specific explanations or a workable question and source plan; 3 = explanations recognize limits, or the project anticipates evidence gaps and a realistic response.
4. **Usability:** 0 = records unusable; 1 = hard to follow or incomplete; 2 = organized enough to retrieve evidence or draft from; 3 = concise, consistently labeled records with a clear next action.

### Rubric D: study plan and final reflection

1. **Coverage:** 0 = required questions absent; 1 = only some addressed; 2 = all addressed; 3 = all addressed in a connected, purposeful account.
2. **Specificity:** 0 = no concrete example or action; 1 = generic statements; 2 = specific goals/actions for the plan or course examples for reflection; 3 = specific, well-chosen details explain why the goals or learning matter.
3. **Self-assessment:** 0 = no reasoning about learning; 1 = unsupported claims about progress; 2 = a reasoned account of a need or change; 3 = balanced account of strengths, limits, and evidence. Personal beliefs and intimate disclosure are never assessment criteria.
4. **Next steps:** 0 = none; 1 = vague intention; 2 = feasible next action; 3 = feasible action tied to an identified need, with a way to check progress.

### Self-review: the default route

1. Reread the activity brief and checklist. Select A, B, C, or D as directed by the task’s work aid.
2. If possible, take a break before reviewing. Read your work as a skeptical but fair reader.
3. Score the four criteria using the exact anchors. Give a draft location and reason for each; use Not assessable when you cannot check a source yet.
4. Choose one or two changes with the greatest effect on the argument. Verify evidence, revise, then recheck the affected criteria.
5. Save the review/revision record. Continue when the required output is present, citations are checked, and you can explain your answer. If uncertainty remains, state it and decide what to revisit. A high score alone does not establish readiness.
