# PHIL 101 midterm answer guide

**Open after saving your independent attempt.** This guide provides criteria and possible lines of response. Other accurate, well-reasoned answers can receive full credit. Correct your work in `04 Midterm/Answer Guide and Corrections`.

## Short-answer guidance

Score each response out of 6: central idea 0–2, explanation 0–2, and relevant example/objection/implication 0–2. Use 1 point for a partly accurate or incomplete element; use 0 for an absent or mistaken element.

1. **Philosophical inquiry:** A question may concern concepts, justification, value, or assumptions not settled by one observation. For example, observing a punishment does not by itself establish its justice. Empirical facts can still matter to a philosophical argument. Avoid claiming philosophy never uses evidence.
2. **Validity and soundness:** An argument is valid when its premises cannot all be true while its conclusion is false. Soundness adds true premises. “All birds are mammals; all robins are birds; therefore all robins are mammals” is structurally valid but unsound. Explain the false premise rather than confusing a false conclusion with invalid form.
3. **Definition and example:** An instance does not state what makes all relevant cases pious. Socrates seeks a general account that can distinguish pious from non-pious cases. Explain why an example can be useful without being sufficient as a definition.
4. **The dilemma:** Are pious acts loved because they are pious, or pious because they are loved? If approval responds to piety, approval does not explain its nature. Agreement removes disagreement among gods but need not remove this explanatory issue. A modern moral extension must be identified as an extension.
5. **Methodical doubt:** Descartes temporarily withholds assent where grounds for doubt can be found, seeking a secure starting point. Dreaming or a deceiver challenges a class of beliefs. The exercise's argumentative role matters; he is not merely listing personal anxieties or endorsing skepticism as his final position.
6. **Cogito:** The occurrence of doubting/thinking cannot occur without the doubter/thinker existing in that act. This does not alone establish an external world, an enduring personal biography, or the full mind–body distinction. Credit careful accounts of the inference or immediate certainty in context.
7. **Freedom:** Determinism concerns how events follow from prior conditions and laws. Fatalism claims an outcome will occur regardless of relevant choices or actions. A compatibilist holds that an appropriate kind of free action can coexist with determinism, often distinguishing action from one's reasons/desires from coercion. Explain rather than merely name the positions.
8. **Knowledge:** The traditional account requires belief, truth, and justification. A belief may be true through luck even when some justification is present, suggesting those conditions need refinement. A coherent example of epistemic luck matters more than naming a particular counterexample.
9. **Induction:** Observed cases do not deductively entail unobserved ones. An appeal to past reliability assumes that relevant past patterns support future reliability, using the kind of inference being defended. Custom helps explain why expectations arise; whether that supplies the desired justification needs an argument.
10. **Arguments about God:** Accept a recognizable assigned cosmological or design argument with explicit premises. A challenge may concern a causal principle, a move from order to a designer, an analogy, or the move from a first cause/designer to specific divine attributes. Credit only objections connected to the argument actually reconstructed; neither belief nor disbelief alone earns points.

For disputed interpretations, return to the assigned primary text and explain the passage supporting your reading. Useful source checks: [Euthyphro](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1642/pg1642-images.html), [Descartes' Meditations](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/descartes1641.pdf), [Hume's Enquiry, Sections IV–V](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/9662/pg9662-images.html), and [OpenStax Chapter 5](https://openstax.org/books/introduction-philosophy/pages/5-introduction).

## Essay guidance

| Criterion | Full credit | Partial credit | Little/no credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thesis / 4 | Focused, defensible answer | Broad or shifting position | No position |
| Reconstruction / 12 | Accurate premises, conclusion, and context | Correct outline with a significant gap | Major distortion or no reconstruction |
| Evaluation / 12 | Serious objection, charitable reply, reasoned judgment | Objection or reply needs development | Mere agreement/disagreement |
| Assigned ideas / 8 | Relevant ideas support the reasoning | Examples or references are vague | Ideas are absent or misused |
| Clarity / 4 | Organized, readable argument | Some repetition or unclear transitions | Reasoning is difficult to follow |

For a partial response, assign points proportionally and write the reason. Do not score by whether the conclusion agrees with this guide.

### Prompt 1

A strong response identifies a particular step rather than treating all six Meditations as one claim. It distinguishes what that step establishes from what remains to be established. Possible objections target the scope of certainty, a premise concerning ideas, or an inference. Evaluate the specific argument reconstructed.

### Prompt 2

A strong response separates the logical gap between past and future, the circularity challenge, and custom's explanatory role. A defense of induction needs to state what standard of justification it offers. A skeptical conclusion should consider the costs and limits of its own position.

### Prompt 3

A strong response explains the direction-of-explanation distinction, then evaluates a proposed account. It distinguishes the ancient dialogue's piety question from any modern moral/theological claim. An objection should challenge a premise or demand for definition, with a reasoned reply.

## Correction and follow-up

1. Total Part A / 60 and Part B / 40. Record a reason for each deduction.
2. For every missed idea, write a corrected account with a source reference.
3. Choose two corrected ideas to explain without notes during next week's review.
4. Record a question you still cannot settle. This is useful evidence of where your inquiry should continue.
