# PHIL 101: Introduction to Philosophy

Version 3 · 15 weeks · 125.25–156.75 h of required work

A question-led introduction to philosophy, focused primarily on the Western tradition. Read primary texts, reconstruct arguments, test objections, and develop your own position. Write and save your work in your own document workspace.

**All required materials are freely available online.** No purchase or paid subscription is needed.

This is an independent study plan. It does not award academic credit or a credential. The workload averages 9.4 hours per week; use the weekly ranges to plan your time. Stretch a week when a text needs more attention. Optional work is excluded from those totals.

## What you will learn

- Identify central questions in metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, religion, and political philosophy.
- Reconstruct premises, conclusions, and assumptions in a primary text.
- Evaluate reasoning using counterexamples and charitable objections.
- Compare competing approaches to a shared philosophical question.
- Defend a position in clear prose with passage references, objections, and replies.

## How to study

Read once for the question and argument, then return to difficult passages. Primary-reading task estimates include an initial close reading; the separate consolidation task is for rereading, argument maps, and notes. Readings and lecture tasks have distinct outputs. Use a short study session when helpful and record time honestly; a timer is a planning aid, not proof of learning.

At the end of each week, recall one argument without your notes, compare it with the source, and correct it. Record a changed belief or uncertainty in your external learning journal. The study guide includes original argument exercises, model reasoning, and a self-review rubric. Open it from an exercise task.

## Assessments and feedback

- 9 required reading responses (plus 1 optional)
- 2 essays in Weeks 4, 10, with outline tasks beforehand.
- Midterm assessment in Weeks 7, 8, 9: 3.75 h.
- Final paper, developed across Weeks 9–15: 20 h across proposal, source notes, outline, draft, feedback, revision, and final submission.
- Weekly reading notes and recall/reflection records.

Self-study needs a feedback loop. Before marking written work complete, check the task rubric, find the strongest objection, and revise a weak paragraph. If a peer or tutor is available, ask them to challenge a specific argument; otherwise, use the delayed self-review method. Checkmarks document completion, not mastery.

## Source and edition notes

The core text is OpenStax *Introduction to Philosophy*. Public-domain primary texts use the editions linked in each task; pagination and translation vary. Cite section, book, chapter, meditation, or passage identifiers where possible. The Descartes link is Jonathan Bennett's modern-language adaptation at Early Modern Texts, not a historical translation. Crash Course links point to individual official episode pages. All required texts are freely available online; see the access notice above.

This course concentrates on a Western introductory canon. It is a foundation for further study, not a survey of all philosophical traditions. OpenStax provides some comparative context; a subsequent course should give sustained attention to traditions underrepresented here.
